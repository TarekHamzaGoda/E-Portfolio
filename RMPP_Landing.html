Unit 1 Discussion: Codes of Ethics and Professional Conduct

Dark UX Patterns are deceptive user experience design techniques that aims to manipulate users into purchasing services that are not in their best interest (ACM, n.d.) The ACM case study reveals an instance in which a developer receives a change request from a client to modify button designs in a manner that confuses the users. Although concerns regarding the misleading nature of the design was raised by the developer, company management decides to proceed with the changes. As a result, the company experiences a notable increase in revenue, against a slight rise in refund requests and customer complaints regarding misleading button designs (ACM, n.d.)

From an ethical standpoint, multiple stakeholders bear responsibility for conducting unethical practices including the Web Developer. According to the ACM Code of Ethics, these designs were intentionally modified to mislead users, causing financial harm by charging for services users did not intend to purchase, violating Principle 1.2 “Avoid Harm”. Additionally, the misleading designs particularly places visually impaired users at a disadvantage, as the button functions new design lacked clarity. As a result, principle 1.4 “Take Action Against Discrimination” is violated as the button functions imposes accessibility challenges (ACM, 2018).

Similarly, the British Computer Society (BCS) Code of Conduct also identifies several breaches. The company failed to consider users’ financial well-being by the intentional implementation of deceptive UX patterns caused users to make unintended purchases, constituting deliberate harm, both are violations of Sections 1a and 2f of the BCS (BCS, 2022).

The developer, despite recognizing the ethical issue, proceeded with implementing the misleading design, thereby failing to act in the public’s best interest. Additionally, the developer did not reject unethical pressure from the client, failing to uphold professional integrity violating both Section 2a: “Professional Competence and Integrity” and Section 2g: “Rejection of Inducements” of the BCS (BCS, 2022).

Unit 1 Reflective Activity: Global Implications of Generative AI Governance Reflective Analysis.

While AI itself is not a new phenomenon, the acceleration of its capabilities has rabidly reshaped industries and professions since 2022. This has raised profound questions about ethics, governance and professional responsibility which necessitates a reconsideration of ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks. This reflective piece illustrates the challenges in establishing AI governance globally, using insights from Correa et al. (2023) and Deckard (2023), and proposes a framework for responsible AI deployment.

The complexities of establishing a global generative AI governance are due to the variety of interests and needs of stakeholders worldwide. Correa et al. (2023) discusses that AI regulations are approached differently by each country and organization based on their economic needs, cultural perspectives and security considerations. For instance, among other countries, the United States regulatory recommendations fall towards the innovative and self-commitment guidelines style with little practical implementation. However, the European Union’s AI Act prioritizes stringent risk-based regulations (Veale & Zuiderveen Borgesius, 2021).

Ethical Challenges of AI

Countries and organizations approach AI regulation differently based on economic priorities, cultural perspectives, and national security considerations. This diversity creates fragmentation in governance, making it difficult to establish a global harmony (Floridi, 2019). Without uniform regulations, companies may exploit regulations, leading to ethical and legal loopholes. Furthermore, AI governance must balance innovation with the mitigation of harms such as bias, misinformation, and privacy violations. In addition, AI poses ethical dilemmas concerning accountability and job displacement that should also be equally addressed (Binns, 2018). AI-generated content can perpetuate biases present in training data, raising concerns about fairness and discrimination. In that sense, AI's ability to create deepfakes and synthetic media complicates trust in information, influencing politics, security, and personal reputation.

Legal and Professional Challenges of AI

Traditional notions of liability and intellectual property (IP) might no longer be applicable to AI. For instance, who is responsible for AI-generated misinformation? Current laws struggle to address accountability when AI systems act unpredictably (Pagallo, 2013). Similarly, copyright laws face challenges in defining ownership of AI-generated content, leading to legal disputes over creative and commercial works (Samuelson, 2022). Nonetheless, computing professional must adhere to the ethical codes of AI governance such those set by the ACM and IEEE. Practitioners must advocate for transparency, fairness, and accountability in AI development while ensuring compliance with evolving regulations.

Recommended Actions

Given the complexities posed by AI governance, several approaches are proposed:

A. Global AI Framework Alignment: International collaboration is crucial to avoid regulatory inconsistencies. Initiatives like the OECD AI Principles and UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021) should be expanded to establish baseline global standards for AI ethics and governance. These principles advocate for inclusiveness, accountability, and respect for human rights, which can serve as a foundation for international dialogue and policy alignment.

B. Context-Sensitive Regulation: While global alignment is important, AI policies must be adaptable to reflect regional legal traditions and cultural values. For instance, China’s approach emphasizes state oversight and data sovereignty, contrasting with the EU’s human-centric approach (Wagner, 2018). Adopting a risk-based approach, as proposed in the EU AI Act, allows regulation to focus on high-risk applications such as facial recognition or autonomous weapons, while enabling experimentation and innovation in safer domains.

C. Transparency Standards: To ensure accountability and foster trust, AI systems should be required to provide explanations of their decision-making processes. Standards for algorithmic transparency and documentation, such as model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) and datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al., 2018), should be adopted widely. These tools help stakeholders assess potential risks and biases in AI systems.

D. Workforce Transition Policies: Governments and industries must proactively manage the socioeconomic impact of AI through investment in education and training. Initiatives like Finland’s Elements of AI course and Singapore’s SkillsFuture program demonstrate effective models for building AI literacy and facilitating career transitions (European Commission, 2020). A global coalition for AI workforce development could standardize best practices and share resources.

E. Public Awareness and Education: There is a growing need for public understanding of AI’s capabilities and limitations to prevent misinformation and promote informed civic participation. Educational campaigns, public consultations, and inclusion of AI ethics in school curricula can empower citizens to engage with AI responsibly. This is especially vital in democracies where public opinion influences regulatory agendas (Cave & Dihal, 2019).

F. Stronger Legal Frameworks for AI Liability: Existing legal systems must evolve to clearly define accountability in AI applications. The European Commission’s proposal for an AI Liability Directive (2022) is a step in the right direction, aiming to ensure victims can claim compensation for damages caused by AI systems. IP laws must also be updated to address questions of authorship and ownership in AI-generated works, possibly introducing a new category of rights for non-human creators.

conclusion

Generative AI presents both opportunities and risks, making governance a critical priority for policymakers, professionals, and society. While establishing a global consensus remains challenging, a balanced approach that promotes innovation while safeguarding ethical and social values is essential. By fostering collaboration, transparency, and proactive regulation, we can navigate the AI revolution responsibly and equitably.

The impact of these actions extends beyond legal compliance; they shape societal trust, professional integrity, and the future of AI-driven progress. As computing professionals, we must actively engage in ethical discourse and governance discussions to ensure AI serves humanity’s best interests.

Unit 3 Discussion Summery: Codes of Ethics and Professional Conduct

This case underscores the broader ethical responsibility of computing professionals to resist unethical design requests and advocate for transparency in UX design. Ethical decision-making should take precedence over brief financial gains, ensuring fairness, accessibility, and trust in digital products. Both the ACM and BCS codes emphasize the duty of computing professionals to prioritize user welfare and integrity, reinforcing the necessity of ethical accountability in UX design. While practices as such violates ethical standards, when developers feel obligated to comply due to job security concerns.

Additionally, client pressure to create unfaithful systems adds another layer of ethical conflict. Businesses often prioritize revenue over ethical considerations, pressuring developers and designers to build interfaces that exploit cognitive biases, obscure key information, or mislead users into unintended actions. While the client may argue that these strategies are common in the industry, ethical computing professionals must challenge these requests and advocate for user-centric design that upholds transparency and fairness. Similarly, other ethical standards such as the User Experience Professionals Association (UXPA) Code of Professional Conduct emphasizes that practitioners should "not knowingly mislead a client or potential client about the suitability of a product or service" (UXPA, n.d.) emphasising on the moral obligations developers have against practices as such.

Key ethical violations:

  • Principle 1.2 (Avoid Harm): Deceptive UX practices mislead users, causing unintended financial losses.
  • Principle 1.4 (Take Action Against Discrimination): The design disproportionately affects visually impaired users, violating accessibility and fairness standards.
  • BCS Code of Conduct (Professional Competence and Integrity): Developers must reject unethical pressures and prioritize user protection over business interests.

    • Furthermore, peers’ responses also emphasized that once a developer recognizes an ethical violation, their obligation extends beyond mere acknowledgment. If management refuses to address concerns, the ethical course of action is to escalate the issue or report the practice. Many deceptive digital practices, especially from smaller companies, go unnoticed, reinforcing the need for professionals to take a stand.

    In conclusion, computing professionals must ensure ethical UX design by resisting unethical directives, advocating for transparency, and protecting users from manipulative design strategies.

    Unit 5 Reflective Activity: The 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal

    The 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal revealed the unethical use of online surveys that aimed to harvest personal data. Through seemingly harmless personality quizzes on Facebook, Cambridge Analytica acquired data from over 87 million users, not only from direct participants but also from their friends, often without explicit consent (Confessore, 2018). This data was then used for targeted political advertising, influencing the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the Brexit referendum (Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison, 2018). At the time, many users were unaware that their personal information was used for political profiling and manipulation.

    Similarly, in 2023, TikTok was fined £12.7 million by the UK’s Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) for misusing children's data. That is because TikTok failed to ensure parental consent for underaged users processed their data for profiling and advertising (ICO, 2023). Unlike traditional surveys, this highlights how data collected via interaction with digital platforms can be inappropriately exploited.

    Another instance of unethical conduct of surveys is the "push polls" in U.S. politics that was used during the 2000 Republican primaries against John McCain. The survey contained misleading questions with the intent of influencing voters under the guise of research (AAPOR, 2020).

    Ethically, these cases violate principles of transparency and informed consent. Socially, they erode trust in media and technology, contributing to misinformation and polarisation. Legally, these practices often breach data protection laws such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the UK Data Protection Act 2018. Professionally, they contradict standards from organisations like the Market Research Society, which emphasise respondent confidentiality and ethical practice (MRS, 2023).

    These incidents demonstrate how surveys and data collection tools, when misused, become powerful instruments of exploitation, calling for stricter oversight and ethical accountability in digital data practices.

    Dicussion Summery: Factors Affecting User Experience

    Managing user reactions from a software and UX perspective and as a Software Project Manager mainly involves addressing user functionality and interface design. According to Mahlke and Thuring (2007) acceptance model for user experience, technology acceptance was determined by variables such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Additionally, characteristics such as terminology, navigation and screen design played a critical role in the over satisfaction of users. Managing user reactions in this case involves a combination of proactive strategies, continuous improvement, and effective communication. Shengli Deng and Yuling Fang (2015) Provides a guide that can assist in managing user emotional experience:

  • Establishing a Communication Channel: Ensures the accessibility to user feedback and concerns. This can be implemented by providing user-friendly support systems, encouraging user to share feedback on their experience through surveys and forums.
  • Active Listing & Feedback Analysis: Listening to user concerns while analysing and identifying patterns and common issues. Implementation includes conducting regular reviews of user feedback and tracking user behaviour (Christian & Malone , 2009).
  • Regular Updates & Continuous Improvements: Keeping user informed of new features, improvements and bug fixes. This can be implemented by highlighting user feedback influence and prioritizing user feedback in development sprints.
  • Setting Realistic Expectations: User expectations about software limitations and capabilities should be manged by clearly communicating scope, realistic timelines and feature implementations.

    • User emotional reactions play a significant role in the success of any software product. As such, project managers must take into account these guidelines to ensure the maximum success of any UX development especially when it comes to functionality and interface design. Therefore, the effects of user emotional reactions can be determined in these areas (Hartson & Pyla , n.d.):

  • User Engagement and Satisfaction: While users that have positive reaction towards a software tend to engage actively and perceive it as valuable, Negative reactions can lead to negative reviews and in extreme cases, abandonment.
  • Brand Perception: Users associate a positive experience with the brand as a whole. However, frustration resulting from negative experience can result in overall brand dissatisfaction that can affect the trust and loyalty.

    • In conclusion. addressing user functionality and interface design with a focus on eliciting positive emotional reactions is crucial for the overall success and sustainability of a software product. As a project Manager, implementing these strategies can actively control user emotions.

    E-Portfolio Activity: Scope Creep

    Scope creep is the gradual expansion of modification of a project’s initial objectives without the any approval. This occurs when new functionalities are added without any corresponding increase in time constraints, budget or resources. Eventually, scope creeping can lead to project delays, cost overruns.

    What aspects of a project are dependent on scope creep?

  • Timeline: changes in objectives can lead to delays and extended timeline
  • Budget: Increased resource requirement leading to budget overruns
  • Quality: Rushed implementation as result of scope creeping can compromise the overall quality

    • How to balance the competing requirements when scope creep occurs?

    There are several strategies to for balancing requirements during scope creep that includes:

  • Prioritization: Defining most critical features that meets project objectives
  • Impact Assessment: Evaluate the impact of proposed changes on timeline and budget.
  • Trade-offs: Identify area where new feature can replace agreed features.

    • How do you stop scope creep?

  • Comprehensive Planning: Invest time in thorough project planning. Clearly define project objectives, requirements, and deliverables upfront.
  • Regular Monitoring: Continuously monitor the project against the original plan. If deviations occur, address them promptly and involve stakeholders in decision-making.
  • Educate Stakeholders: Ensure that all stakeholders understand the potential consequences of scope changes, including impacts on timeline, budget, and project quality.

    E-Portfolio Activity: Software Quality Factors

    Software quality factors has evolved significantly since the 1970’s as a result of newer considerations and technological developments. This contributed significantly to the software development standards in many areas. In an article by (Lee, 2014) that discusses current software quality metrics, several factors are compared against McCall’s paper (McCall et al., 1977) during the 1970’s:

    Complexity and Scale:

  • 1970s: Software projects were often smaller in scale and less complex. The systems developed were typically standalone applications, and the development processes were less formalized.
  • 1970s: Software projects were often smaller in scale and less complex. The systems developed were typically standalone applications, and the development processes were less formalized.

    • Development Methodologies:

  • 1970s: Waterfall model and other traditional, linear methodologies were commonly used. Development was often a sequential process with little room for iteration.
  • Today: Agile methodologies, DevOps, and continuous integration are widespread. Iterative and incremental development processes allow for more flexibility, adaptability to changing requirements, and continuous improvement.

    • 3. Security:

  • 1970s: Security concerns were less prominent, and there was a limited awareness of potential vulnerabilities. Security measures were often an afterthought.
  • Today: Security is a top priority. Software development practices include security considerations from the early stages, and there is a focus on implementing best practices to protect against cyber threats and vulnerabilities.

    • Availability and Reliability:

  • 1970s: System availability and reliability were important but were sometimes compromised due to hardware limitations and less robust software design.
  • Today: High availability and reliability are critical, especially in mission-critical systems and cloud-based applications. Redundancy, fault tolerance, and disaster recovery planning are common practices.
